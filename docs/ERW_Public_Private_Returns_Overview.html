The ideaOne practice, two benefits
The idea

One field practice, two benefits.

What is done

Basalt, a common volcanic rock, is crushed to a fine dust and spread on fields with the same equipment used for agricultural lime.

Benefit to the farmer

As the dust dissolves it neutralizes soil acidity, exactly as lime does. Acid soils are a major reason yields stay low, so treating them raises harvests.

Benefit to the climate

The same reaction pulls carbon dioxide out of the air and turns it into dissolved bicarbonate, which drains to rivers and the ocean and stays there for thousands of years.

Why it is different

Lime also fixes acidity but releases its own carbon. Basalt fixes acidity and removes carbon. That second benefit can be sold to carbon-credit buyers.

Why Sub-Saharan Africa

Acid soils are widespread, and rarely treated.

Blue and green on the map are acid soils. They cover the humid belt from West Africa through Central Africa to the highlands of the east.

~23%
of cropland is acid enough to depress yields (about 33 million hectares)
~$6B
yearly value of harvest lost to soil acidity, by recent estimates

Liming is rare because the upfront cost is large and smallholders cannot easily borrow against a benefit that arrives over several seasons. Warm, wet conditions also make basalt dissolve faster here than in temperate regions.

The questionWhere would it pay, and who would pay
The question

Where would it pay, and who would pay for it?

For every 9 km patch of cropland we ask the same three questions, each against the full cost of buying, grinding, hauling and spreading the rock.

The farmer’s case

Does the extra harvest alone cover the cost?

If yes, farmers or input dealers could fund it themselves and no carbon money is needed.

The carbon buyer’s case

Does the carbon removed alone cover the cost?

If yes, a carbon-credit buyer could fund it purely as climate action, with the yield gain as a bonus.

The combined case

Does it pay only when both are added?

If yes, carbon money is the missing piece that turns an unaffordable farm investment into a viable one.

Why the split matters. The three answers point to three different financing routes: private input markets, carbon-credit purchases, and a blend where the credit acts like a cost-share. The map tells us which route fits which place.

How the model worksThree streams, one balance
How the model works

Three streams add up to one balance per patch.

Return per hectare  =  value of extra harvest  +  value of carbon removed  −  delivered cost of rock
Harvest stream

How acid is the soil, which crops grow there, how much each crop responds to acidity relief, and what the crop sells for locally.

Carbon stream

How much carbon a tonne of rock removes in that climate and soil, minus carbon that never leaves the field, valued at a carbon price net of monitoring cost.

Cost stream

Rock at the quarry, electricity to grind it, truck time from the nearest basalt source over real roads, and spreading on the field.

Two ways to dose

Targeted: just enough rock to fix each soil’s acidity, like a lime recommendation. Uniform: a flat 10, 20 or 50 tonnes per hectare everywhere, the convention in the carbon literature.

Two moments in time

First application: a big one-off dose. Steady state: a small yearly top-up once soils are fixed. Headline results use the steady state, where both benefits recur every year.

What comes out

A map of the balance for every patch under four doses and three timings, a ranking of the best patches under yearly rock-supply limits, and a 180-run stress test over prices, costs, yields and carbon rates.

Reading the map

Pale areas sit near basalt sources and get rock cheaply. Dark red areas are far from any source: even with excellent soils and crops, rock there costs too much to move.

Why this matters

Transport is the one cost that varies from place to place, so it decides where the practice is possible. Cheaper haulage, for example electrified trucking, is a real lever.

CarbonHow much a tonne of rock really removes
Carbon

A tonne of rock removes far less than the textbook number.

In theory a tonne of basalt can lock up about 310 kg of carbon dioxide. In a real field, only part of the rock dissolves within a useful time, and hauling and grinding emit some carbon of their own.

What we use

Roughly 80 to 90 kg per tonne in a warm, humid, well-connected place, scaled up or down by local temperature, rainfall and soil acidity. Dry areas lose more because carbon can re-precipitate in the soil.

The biggest unknown

This number is the least-measured quantity in the whole field, especially for tropical smallholder soils. Every result about carbon should be read with that in mind.

CarbonCounting only what leaves the field
Carbon · an accounting rule we added this year

Fixing acidity uses up carbon before any is stored.

The mechanism

The alkalinity that basalt releases can either neutralize soil acidity or drain away as stored carbon. It cannot do both. The part that neutralizes acidity gives its carbon back to the air, exactly as lime does.

The rule

We credit only the carbon that is left over after the soil’s acidity has been paid down. Most global models skip this step; the 2026 systematic review of the field notes it is “not considered in all global and regional model estimates.”

Two consequences

First, the dose that is best for the farmer (just enough to fix the soil) removes almost no carbon. Second, durable removal is mostly a steady-state story: at first application under a third survives, in steady state most of it does.

~70%
of credited carbon disappears at first application once acidity is netted out
77–90%
survives in steady state, when the yearly top-up only offsets fresh acidity

What it means for buyers. The first application is, in carbon terms, mostly liming. The carbon case belongs to programs that keep fields treated year after year.

ResultsWhere carbon alone pays
Results · the carbon buyer’s case

Carbon alone pays on less land, and depends on the price.

At today’s conservative price of $150 per tonne, durable carbon covers the cost on about 2 million hectares with the targeted dose, and about 4 million with a flat 20 t/ha dose that leaves more alkalinity free to drain away.

The price ladder

Carbon-only viability is steeply price-dependent. Between $250 and $350 a large share of treated cropland tips over the line. Current buyers describe $271 as expensive, and suppliers say they need about $349 to be profitable.

What it costs to remove a tonne

Across treated land the typical cost of one durably removed tonne is about $276 with the flat dose and $330 with the targeted dose, against a net price of $130 after monitoring. Only the cheapest patches clear today.

ResultsWhat would change the picture
Results · what would change the picture

Two levers move the carbon case; one moves the farmer’s.

1 · Carbon removed per tonne of rock

The strongest lever. If a tonne of rock verifiably removed twice as much carbon, the land where carbon alone pays would grow about six-fold. Better measurement raises this number as much as better rock does.

2 · Carbon price relative to delivered cost

Price, monitoring cost and delivered cost act as one lever, through their ratio. A higher price or cheaper rock both help, but less than lever 1 for large moves.

3 · Cheaper haulage

Cutting transport cost by a quarter, for instance with solar-electric trucking, expands the farmer’s case from 4.3 to 5.1 Mha but barely moves the carbon case. Logistics helps farmers; price and measurement help buyers.

ProgressWhere the field stands
Progress · what the 2026 systematic review tells us

The field agrees on the question we are asking.

Not whether, but where

The review of 241 studies concludes that the question is no longer whether rock weathering works, but “where, when, and how it can be made to work most effectively.” That is exactly what a map answers.

Yield benefits are real

Across 166 agronomic studies, 129 report gains and 11 losses. Field trials in maize show 10 to 70% more harvest, and the response depends on relieving a soil constraint, not on piling on more rock.

Carbon numbers are wide

Measured removal rates span several orders of magnitude, with a median of 0.84 tonnes per hectare per year, and tend to sit below what global projections assume.

The market is small

About 0.8 million tonnes of rock-weathering credits have been sold, 1 to 2% of all durable removal credits, mostly to two buyers. Few tonnes have been delivered yet.

Policy points the same way

The review calls for subsidies “where on-farm benefits do not fully cover the cost” and for folding rock dust into existing liming subsidies. Our combined-only band is the map of where that condition holds.

Africa is under-studied

Research is led from the UK, US and Germany; large parts of Africa are “sparsely represented,” even though the highest potential is in the tropics. Our model runs on the least-measured region.

Suhrhoff et al. (2026), An Ecosystem of Carbon Dioxide Removal Reviews, Part 3: Enhanced Weathering. CDRXIV preprint 417, submitted to Energy & Environmental Science, not yet peer reviewed.

CaveatsWhat the model leaves out
Caveats

What the model does not yet do.

Who pays upfront

We map where returns cover costs, not who can finance the first dose. Tenure, credit and cost-sharing are not modelled.

Thin yield evidence

The crop response rests on a dozen or so trials plus a crop-suitability model. Reassuringly, the map is least sensitive to this input.

Carbon lost downstream

Some stored carbon escapes in rivers and at the ocean surface, roughly 15 to 20% in published estimates. We do not deduct it, so carbon results lean slightly high.

Carbon that arrives late

Soils can hold weathered material for years before it drains. We treat that as a conservative deduction; some of it will eventually count.

Other greenhouse gases

Most studies find rock dust reduces nitrous oxide from fields. We leave this out, which works against the practice, not for it.

Farm counts

Derived from national average holding sizes, so they are order-of-magnitude indications, not enumerations.

How to read the results

The ordering of the findings is robust: yield pays on more land than carbon, the combined-only band is the largest opportunity, and the core geography holds across the stress test. The absolute carbon numbers move with the removal rate per tonne, the input we most need to measure.

TakeawaysFour things to remember
Takeaways

Four things to remember.

1

The two benefits rarely pay on their own

But together they make about 2.7 million hectares of acid cropland worth treating that neither would alone.

2

Carbon money is the missing margin, not a bonus

On most viable land the credit is what makes a yield-improving investment affordable. That is a cost-share for farmers, and a clearly additional credit for buyers.

3

The carbon case hinges on one number

How much CO₂ a tonne of rock verifiably removes. It is the least-measured quantity in the field and the first thing to go and measure.

4

There is a place to start

The Cameroon and Guinea highlands stay attractive under most assumptions. Pilots there would test the model where a result is most likely to change a decision.
